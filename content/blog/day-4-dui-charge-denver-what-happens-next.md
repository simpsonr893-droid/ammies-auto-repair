# Charged with a DUI in Denver? Here's What Happens Next

A DUI arrest is disorienting: one traffic stop, and suddenly you're facing a criminal case, a license problem, and a lot of unknowns. The most important thing to understand is that you are actually facing **two separate cases** — one criminal, one administrative — and the administrative one has a deadline measured in days, not months.

## Act fast: the DMV clock is already running

If you took a breath test over the limit or refused testing, the Colorado DMV process to revoke your license starts immediately — separate from your criminal case, and it moves much faster. You have only a **short window (seven days, in the typical breath-test or refusal scenario)** to request a DMV **Express Consent hearing** to challenge the revocation. Miss it, and the revocation proceeds automatically regardless of what happens in criminal court. Requesting the hearing is often worth it even in difficult cases: it preserves options, and it gives your lawyer an early chance to question the officer under oath.

This deadline is the single best reason to talk to a DUI lawyer within days of the arrest, not weeks.

## DUI vs. DWAI: Colorado's two-tier system

Colorado has two impaired-driving offenses:

- **DUI** — driving under the influence, with a per se version at blood/breath alcohol of **0.08 or more**.
- **DWAI** — driving while ability impaired, a lesser offense that can apply at **0.05 to under 0.08**, when impairment "to the slightest degree" is shown.

Both are criminal offenses that go on your record. Prosecutions can also rest on drugs — including marijuana, where a blood THC level of 5 ng/mL permits an inference of impairment — or any combination.

## What penalties look like

For a **first offense** with no aggravating factors, a Colorado DUI is a misdemeanor typically involving possible jail (often suspended or converted to probation on a first offense), fines and costs, community service, license revocation, alcohol education classes, and — very commonly — an **ignition interlock** requirement to drive again during a restricted period. A high BAC (0.15+) triggers "persistent drunk driver" consequences even on a first offense, including a longer interlock period and mandatory treatment.

Penalties escalate sharply with priors: Colorado counts prior impaired-driving convictions from your **entire lifetime**, anywhere in the country, and a **fourth offense can be charged as a felony**. A DUI involving injury or death is a felony from the start.

Beyond the courtroom, a conviction ripples outward: insurance premiums, employment (especially any job involving driving), professional licenses, and immigration consequences for non-citizens. Impaired-driving convictions in Colorado generally **cannot be sealed**, which raises the stakes of fighting the case properly the first time.

## How DUI cases actually get defended

Pleading guilty at the first opportunity is rarely the smart move — not because every case beats the charge, but because the facts are rarely as airtight as they first appear. Experienced defense lawyers examine:

- **The stop:** did the officer have a lawful basis to pull you over?
- **Roadside tests:** field sobriety tests are voluntary in Colorado, subjective, and frequently administered improperly.
- **The chemical test:** breath machines require calibration and maintenance records; blood samples have chain-of-custody and lab-procedure requirements. Discrepancies here have unwound many cases.
- **The timeline:** rising-BAC arguments and delays between driving and testing.

Even where the evidence is strong, counsel can often negotiate the outcome — for example, a reduction from DUI to DWAI, or a plea structure that minimizes jail exposure and preserves driving ability through interlock — and steer you through probation terms so a first offense stays a one-time event.

## What to do this week

1. Write down everything you remember about the stop while it's fresh — times, locations, what was said, what tests were done.
2. Calendar the DMV hearing deadline and request the hearing (or have your lawyer do it).
3. Don't discuss the case with anyone but a lawyer, and don't post about it.
4. Line up counsel before your first court appearance. Bring your paperwork — the summons, the Express Consent affidavit, and any bond documents.

## FAQ

**Should I have refused the test?**
That decision is behind you either way — but know that refusal carries its own consequences: automatic revocation, designation as a persistent drunk driver with interlock and treatment requirements, and the refusal can be used against you at trial.

**Can I drive right now?**
It depends on your paperwork — an affidavit often serves as a temporary permit for a short period. This is a specific, personal question to resolve with a lawyer or the DMV *before* you drive.

**Is a public defender an option?**
Public defenders are real, skilled lawyers, but they're available only if you financially qualify, and they can't represent you at the DMV hearing. Many private DUI lawyers offer payment plans and flat fees.
