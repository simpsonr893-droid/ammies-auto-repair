# Filing for Divorce in Colorado: What Denver Residents Need to Know

Divorce is one of the most stressful legal processes most people will ever go through — and much of that stress comes from not knowing what happens next. This guide walks through how divorce actually works in Colorado, from filing to final decree, so you can make decisions from information rather than fear.

## Colorado is a no-fault state

Colorado courts grant divorce on a single ground: the marriage is **irretrievably broken**. Nobody has to prove cheating, cruelty, or abandonment, and with narrow exceptions, misconduct doesn't change how property is divided. This surprises many people — the energy spent proving a spouse "caused" the divorce is usually better spent on the issues courts actually decide: property, parenting, and support.

## The basic requirements and timeline

- **Residency:** at least one spouse must have lived in Colorado for **91 days** before filing.
- **Where to file:** in the district court of the county where either spouse lives — for the city and county of Denver, that's Denver District Court.
- **The waiting period:** a Colorado court cannot enter a divorce decree until at least **91 days** after the case starts (filing jointly, or serving the other spouse). That's a floor, not an estimate — contested cases routinely take six months to over a year.
- **While the case is pending:** an automatic temporary injunction takes effect, restraining both spouses from things like draining accounts, canceling insurance, or taking the children out of state without consent.

## What the court decides

**Property and debt.** Colorado uses **equitable distribution**: marital property — generally, most of what either spouse acquired during the marriage, regardless of whose name is on it — is divided fairly, which does not always mean 50/50. Separate property (owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance) generally stays with its owner, though the *increase in its value* during the marriage can be marital. Retirement accounts, home equity, and businesses are all on the table, and full financial disclosure from both spouses is mandatory.

**Parenting.** Colorado doesn't use the word "custody" anymore. Courts allocate **parental responsibilities** — parenting time and decision-making authority — based on the child's best interests. Parents are generally required to attempt mediation for disputes, and most cases resolve with an agreed parenting plan rather than a courtroom battle.

**Child support.** Set by a statewide income-shares formula using both parents' incomes, the number of overnights, and costs like health insurance and childcare. It's math more than argument — but the inputs (what counts as income, how many overnights) are where cases are won and lost.

**Spousal maintenance.** Colorado has advisory guidelines that suggest maintenance amounts and durations based on incomes and length of marriage. Judges use them as a starting point, not a mandate.

## Do you need a lawyer?

Colorado permits self-represented divorce, and the courts publish standard forms. A DIY divorce can work when the marriage is short, assets are simple, and both spouses agree on everything. Strongly consider a lawyer when any of these are present:

- Children, and any disagreement about parenting time
- A house, retirement accounts, or a business
- A large income gap between spouses
- Any history of domestic violence or financial control
- A spouse who has already hired counsel

Many Denver family lawyers also offer **limited-scope ("unbundled") representation** — paying for help with specific tasks, like reviewing a settlement agreement or coaching you for a hearing — which can dramatically lower the cost of getting real advice.

## Protecting yourself early

Before or immediately after filing: gather financial records (tax returns, pay stubs, account statements), inventory major assets and debts, open individual accounts for your own paychecks going forward (consistent with the injunction's rules), and change passwords on your personal email and devices. Do **not** hide assets, record your spouse illegally, or vent about the case on social media — all three reliably backfire in court.

## FAQ

**How much does divorce cost in Colorado?**
Filing fees are a few hundred dollars. Total cost depends almost entirely on conflict: an uncontested divorce may cost little beyond the filing fee, while a fully contested case with experts can reach tens of thousands. Settlement is a financial strategy, not a surrender.

**Does it matter who files first?**
Legally, very little. Practically, filing first lets you prepare on your own timeline.

**Can we use the same lawyer to save money?**
One lawyer cannot represent both spouses. In amicable cases, one spouse's lawyer can draft documents while the other consults independent counsel to review — or you can use a trained mediator, plus review attorneys.
