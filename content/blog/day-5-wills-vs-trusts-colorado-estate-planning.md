# Wills vs. Trusts in Colorado: Which One Does Your Family Need?

Most Coloradans know they should "get their affairs in order," and most put it off — often because the choices seem confusing and the sales pitches contradictory. Trust seminars insist probate is a nightmare to be avoided at all costs; skeptics say a simple will is all anyone needs. The truth, as usual, is in the middle — and it depends on Colorado law, which is friendlier to probate than most states.

## What a will does

A **will** is your written instruction for what happens after death: who receives your property, who administers your estate (the *personal representative*), and — critically for parents — who you nominate as **guardian for minor children**. A will only takes effect at death, and it must pass through **probate** to move property.

Colorado's execution requirements are straightforward: a written document, signed by you, and either signed by two witnesses or notarized. Colorado even recognizes holographic (handwritten) wills in some circumstances — though "legally valid" and "well-drafted" are very different things.

## The good news about Colorado probate

Here's what the trust seminars don't emphasize: **Colorado has one of the simpler probate systems in the country.** As an adopter of the Uniform Probate Code, Colorado offers:

- **Small-estate affidavit:** modest estates with no real estate (under a dollar threshold that adjusts periodically — in the ballpark of $80,000+) can skip court entirely.
- **Informal probate:** the standard path for uncontested estates — largely paperwork-driven, without court hearings, with the personal representative administering the estate with minimal supervision.
- **Formal probate:** reserved for disputes or complications.

A typical informal probate is measured in months, not years, and costs far less than horror stories suggest. Probate is public, and it does take time — but for many families it is not the catastrophe it's marketed as.

## What a revocable living trust does

A **revocable living trust** is a container you create during life. You transfer assets into it, remain in full control as trustee, and name a successor trustee to take over at incapacity or death. Property properly held in the trust **passes outside probate** according to the trust's terms.

Trusts genuinely shine when you have:

- **Real estate in more than one state** — a trust avoids running a separate probate in each state, which is the scenario where probate avoidance saves real money.
- **Privacy concerns** — trusts are not public records; probated wills are.
- **Incapacity planning needs** — a successor trustee can manage trust assets seamlessly if you develop dementia or become disabled, often avoiding a court conservatorship.
- **Beneficiaries who shouldn't inherit outright** — minor children, a family member with special needs (where an inheritance could disrupt benefits), or heirs who need distributions staged over time.
- **Blended families** — a trust can provide for a current spouse while guaranteeing that assets ultimately reach children from a prior marriage.

The trade-offs: a trust costs more to set up, and it only works if you actually **fund it** — retitling your house, accounts, and other assets into the trust's name. Unfunded trusts are the most common estate-planning failure; the beautiful binder on the shelf does nothing for assets still titled in your personal name. (A "pour-over will" catches strays, but those assets still go through probate.)

## The tools everyone needs regardless

Whichever route you choose, a complete Colorado estate plan includes:

1. **Financial power of attorney** — someone to handle money and property if you're incapacitated.
2. **Medical durable power of attorney** — someone to make healthcare decisions.
3. **Living will (advance directive)** — your end-of-life treatment wishes.
4. **Beneficiary designations, reviewed** — retirement accounts and life insurance pass by designation, overriding your will. Outdated designations (an ex-spouse, a deceased parent) cause more real-world disasters than badly drafted wills. Colorado also allows **beneficiary deeds** for real estate and payable-on-death designations on accounts — simple probate-avoidance tools worth discussing.

## So: will or trust?

A reasonable rule of thumb for Colorado: a **will-based plan** (will + powers of attorney + reviewed beneficiary designations) serves many single-state, straightforward situations well, precisely because Colorado probate is manageable. A **trust-based plan** earns its cost when you have out-of-state property, privacy or incapacity concerns, a blended family, beneficiaries needing protection, or substantial assets. An estate planning attorney can usually tell you which side of the line you're on within one conversation.

## FAQ

**What happens if I die without any plan?**
Colorado's intestacy statutes decide — a fixed formula favoring your spouse and children, then parents and siblings. It ignores unmarried partners, stepchildren, friends, and charities entirely, and a court chooses your children's guardian without your input.

**Does a trust save estate taxes?**
For most families, no — the federal estate tax only reaches large estates (a threshold in the millions), and Colorado has no state estate or inheritance tax. Revocable trusts are about probate, privacy, and control, not taxes.

**How often should I update my plan?**
Review after any major life event — marriage, divorce, births, deaths, a move, a big change in assets — and every three to five years regardless.
