# Know Your Rights at Work: A Colorado Employee's Guide to Employment Law

Colorado has quietly become one of the most employee-protective states in the country — on wages, transparency, leave, noncompetes, and discrimination. But rights only matter if you know them. Here's a practical tour of what Colorado workers are entitled to in 2026, and what to do when an employer crosses a line.

## At-will employment — and its real limits

Colorado is an at-will state: absent a contract, you can quit, and your employer can fire you, at any time, for almost any reason or no reason. The key word is *almost*. An employer may **not** fire you for:

- **Who you are:** race, color, religion, national origin, ancestry, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, age, disability, marital status, and other protected characteristics under Colorado law — which reaches employers of all sizes, smaller than federal law covers.
- **What you lawfully did:** filing a wage complaint, reporting discrimination or safety violations, taking protected leave, serving on a jury, or engaging in lawful off-duty activities.
- **Refusing to break the law:** firing someone for declining to commit an illegal act violates public policy.

"Wrongful termination" doesn't mean unfair termination — it means termination for one of these illegal reasons. If you suspect it, the evidence is usually in the timeline: strong reviews until you complained, then sudden write-ups. Preserve documents (forward key emails to personal accounts is risky and can violate policy — instead, keep a dated personal journal and note names of witnesses).

## Wages: Colorado's rules beat the federal floor

- **Minimum wage:** Colorado's minimum wage is well above the federal rate and adjusts annually for inflation; **Denver sets its own, higher, local minimum wage.** Check the current posted rates — your employer must display them.
- **Overtime:** Colorado requires time-and-a-half after **40 hours a week**, and — unlike federal law — also after **12 hours in a single workday**. Salaried doesn't automatically mean exempt; exemption depends on duties and a minimum salary threshold.
- **Breaks:** a paid 10-minute rest period every 4 hours and an unpaid 30-minute meal period on shifts over 5 hours, for covered workers.
- **Final paychecks:** owed immediately or on the next regular payday depending on how the separation happened. Colorado's wage law lets employees send a **written demand**; an employer who wrongly refuses can owe **penalties on top of the wages** — often multiples — plus attorney fees. The Colorado Department of Labor and Employment (CDLE) accepts wage complaints without a lawyer.
- **Tips and misclassification:** tips belong to workers, and labeling someone a "1099 contractor" doesn't make it so — the actual working relationship controls, and misclassification claims recover unpaid overtime and benefits.

## Transparency, sick leave, and family leave

- **Pay transparency:** Colorado's Equal Pay for Equal Work Act requires job postings to include compensation ranges and benefits, and gives employees rights around promotion opportunities and pay equity by sex — including the right to sue over unjustified pay gaps.
- **Paid sick leave:** Colorado employers must provide accrued paid sick leave (an hour per 30 worked, up to 48 hours a year), plus supplemental leave during public health emergencies.
- **FAMLI:** Colorado's state paid family and medical leave program pays partial wages for up to 12 weeks (more for pregnancy complications) for bonding with a new child, your own or a family member's serious health condition, safe leave, and more. It's funded by payroll premiums you've already been paying — use it.

## Noncompetes: mostly dead in Colorado

For most workers, noncompete agreements signed today are **void** in Colorado. Only workers above a "highly compensated" earnings threshold can be bound, only with proper advance notice, and only to protect trade secrets — and improper agreements expose employers to penalties. If a departing employer waves an old noncompete at you, have a lawyer look before assuming it's enforceable; the odds are increasingly in your favor.

## If something's wrong: the playbook

1. **Document in real time.** Dates, times, exact words, witnesses. Contemporaneous notes are evidence; memories are not.
2. **Use internal channels once, in writing.** Report discrimination or harassment to HR by email — this both gives the employer its legal chance to fix it and creates the retaliation-protected complaint.
3. **Mind the deadlines.** Discrimination charges must typically be filed with the Colorado Civil Rights Division (CCRD) or EEOC within roughly 300 days; wage claims and other suits have their own statutes of limitations. Deadlines kill more good cases than bad facts do.
4. **Get advice before you sign anything.** Severance agreements trade money for releasing your claims — sometimes fairly, sometimes not. A one-hour consultation before signing is one of the best investments a departing employee can make. Many employment lawyers offer contingency or fee-shifting arrangements for strong claims.

## FAQ

**Can I be fired for discussing my pay with coworkers?**
No — pay discussions are protected under both Colorado and federal law.

**Do I get my unused vacation paid out when I leave?**
Yes. Colorado treats earned vacation as wages that cannot be forfeited — "use it or lose it" policies that take away earned vacation at separation are unenforceable.

**My employer is out of state but I work remotely from Denver. Whose law applies?**
Generally, Colorado's protections follow work performed in Colorado — including its wage rules and posting requirements. This trips up many out-of-state employers, to remote workers' benefit.
