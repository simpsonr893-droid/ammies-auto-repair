# What to Do After a Car Accident in Denver: A Step-by-Step Legal Guide

Between rush hour on I-25, sudden snow on Sixth Avenue, and distracted drivers everywhere, crashes are a daily reality in Denver. What you do in the hours and days after a collision has a direct effect on your health, your finances, and any legal claim you may need to make. Here is the sequence that protects you.

## At the scene

**1. Get safe and call 911.** Colorado law requires you to stop, and to report accidents involving injury, death, or property damage. If anyone might be hurt — even "just shaken up" — ask for medical response. In Denver, police may direct you to file an online report for minor, non-injury crashes; do it the same day.

**2. Document everything.** Photograph vehicle positions before they're moved, all damage, skid marks, traffic signals, weather, and any visible injuries. Get names, phone numbers, insurance details, and license plates. If there are witnesses, get their contact information before they drive away — they are the first thing to disappear from a case.

**3. Say less.** Be polite, exchange information, answer the officer's questions honestly — but don't apologize or speculate about fault at the scene. "I'm sorry, I didn't see you" will appear in an insurance file later. Fault is a legal conclusion that depends on facts you don't have yet.

## The first week

**4. See a doctor even if you feel fine.** Adrenaline masks injuries. Soft-tissue damage, concussions, and back injuries often surface 24–72 hours later. A same-week medical visit protects your health and creates the record that connects your injuries to the crash — the single most common gap insurers exploit.

**5. Notify your own insurer promptly.** Your policy requires timely notice, and your own coverage (MedPay, uninsured/underinsured motorist) may be valuable regardless of fault. Colorado auto policies include MedPay coverage by default unless it was rejected in writing — many people have it without knowing.

**6. Be careful with the other driver's insurer.** The at-fault carrier's adjuster may call quickly, sounding friendly, asking for a recorded statement or offering a fast check. You are not obligated to give a recorded statement to the *other* driver's insurance company, and early settlement offers are almost always calculated before the full extent of your injuries is known. Once you sign a release, the claim is over — even if your condition worsens.

## How fault works in Colorado

Colorado is an at-fault state with **modified comparative negligence**: your compensation is reduced by your percentage of fault, and if you are 50% or more at fault, you recover nothing. This is exactly why insurers work hard to shift blame percentages — a claim moved from 20% to 50% fault is a claim they don't pay. Police reports, photos, witness statements, and sometimes vehicle data or reconstruction experts decide these percentage battles.

## What your claim can include

- Medical bills — past and reasonably expected future treatment
- Lost wages and reduced future earning capacity
- Vehicle damage, rental, and out-of-pocket costs
- Physical pain and emotional distress
- Permanent impairment or disfigurement, where applicable

## When to call a lawyer

You may not need a lawyer for a minor fender-bender with no injuries. Strongly consider one when: anyone was injured beyond first aid; liability is disputed; a commercial vehicle, rideshare, or government vehicle was involved; the at-fault driver was uninsured; or an adjuster is pressuring you to settle. Injury lawyers work on contingency (see our [guide to choosing a personal injury lawyer in Denver](/blog/choose-personal-injury-lawyer-denver)), so the consultation costs nothing.

**Mind the deadline:** claims arising from motor vehicle accidents in Colorado generally carry a three-year statute of limitations, but shorter deadlines apply in special situations — notably claims involving government vehicles or road conditions, which require formal notice within months, not years.

## FAQ

**The other driver has no insurance. Am I out of luck?**
Not necessarily. Your own uninsured motorist (UM) coverage steps into the at-fault driver's shoes. Colorado insurers must offer UM/UIM coverage, and many drivers carry it.

**Should I accept the insurer's first offer?**
Almost never before your medical treatment is complete or your future costs are understood. First offers are opening positions, not valuations.

**What if I was partly at fault?**
You can still recover as long as you were less than 50% at fault — your award is simply reduced by your share. Don't concede a percentage in a phone call; that's a negotiation, not a formality.
