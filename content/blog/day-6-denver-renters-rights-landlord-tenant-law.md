# Denver Renters' Rights: A Practical Guide to Colorado Landlord-Tenant Law

Roughly half of Denver households rent — and Colorado's landlord-tenant laws have changed more in the past five years than in the previous thirty, almost entirely in tenants' favor. Many renters (and plenty of landlords) are still operating on outdated assumptions. Here's where the law actually stands and how to use it.

## Your home must be livable: the warranty of habitability

Every Colorado residential lease carries an implied **warranty of habitability**: functioning heat, running water, hot water, plumbing, electricity, weatherproofing, working locks, and premises free of serious hazards like mold or pest infestations. You cannot sign this away — lease clauses waiving it are unenforceable.

**How to enforce it:** put the problem in writing (email or text counts — and creates the record you'll need), keep photos, and give the landlord reasonable notice to fix it. Colorado law requires landlords to respond promptly, and recent reforms strengthened tenant remedies when they don't — including rent reductions, repair-and-deduct in defined circumstances, lease termination, and damages. What you should *not* do is simply stop paying rent without following the statutory process; unilateral rent-withholding done wrong converts a strong habitability case into an eviction case.

## Security deposits: the one-month rule and the treble-damages hammer

- **Deposit caps:** Colorado now generally caps security deposits at **two months' rent** (with tighter limits in some situations).
- **Return deadline:** after you move out, the landlord must return your deposit or a **written, itemized statement** of deductions within **one month** — unless the lease extends it, to a maximum of about 60 days.
- **Normal wear and tear cannot be deducted.** Faded paint, worn carpet in walkways, small nail holes — that's the landlord's cost of doing business, not yours.
- **The penalty:** a landlord who wrongfully withholds a deposit and blows past the statutory process can be liable for **up to three times** the wrongfully withheld amount, plus attorney fees. A short certified letter citing the deposit statute and demanding return resolves a remarkable number of these disputes; small claims court handles the rest — no lawyer required for most deposit cases.

**Protect yourself:** photograph the unit thoroughly at move-in and move-out, and get a written move-in checklist.

## Rent, fees, and applications

Recent Colorado legislation reined in several long-standing practices: application fees must reflect actual costs, prospective tenants can reuse a recent screening report, late fees are capped and can only be charged per the statute (with required grace-period rules), and a range of junk fees have been restricted. Rent increases are not capped in Colorado, but a landlord generally may raise rent only **once in a 12-month period** and must give substantial advance notice for tenants without a fixed-term lease.

## Eviction: process, not padlocks

A landlord in Colorado can only remove you through **court**. Changing the locks, shutting off utilities, or removing your belongings is an illegal "self-help" eviction with real penalties. The lawful process requires:

1. **Written notice** (commonly a 10-day demand to pay or cure for nonpayment or lease violations, with different periods for other situations);
2. **A court case** (forcible entry and detainer) where you have the right to appear and raise defenses — habitability violations, improper notice, retaliation, and discrimination among them;
3. **A sheriff**, and only a sheriff, executing any final order.

Show up to court — most eviction judgments happen by default because the tenant didn't appear. Denver also has eviction legal defense resources, and Colorado law now provides court-appointed or free counsel pathways for many low-income tenants. Retaliation for asserting your rights (complaining to authorities, requesting repairs, joining a tenant organization) is itself unlawful.

## Discrimination and source of income

Colorado's fair housing protections go beyond the federal baseline: landlords can't discriminate based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability, familial status, marital status — or **source of income**, meaning most landlords can't refuse you simply for using a housing voucher. Complaints can be filed with the Colorado Civil Rights Division.

## When to get a lawyer

Small claims court works fine for deposit disputes. Get legal help when: you've been served an eviction case; habitability problems are serious and the landlord won't act; you suspect discrimination; you're facing a lockout or utility shutoff; or you're organizing with other tenants in the building. Many Denver tenant lawyers take strong cases on fee-shifting statutes (the landlord pays your attorney fees if you win), and legal aid serves income-qualified renters — see our upcoming guide to [free legal aid in Denver](/blog/free-legal-aid-denver).

## FAQ

**Can my landlord enter whenever they want?**
Colorado law now requires reasonable notice for non-emergency entry. Document violations in writing.

**I'm month-to-month. How much notice to end my tenancy?**
Notice periods scale with how long you've lived there — longer tenancies get more notice. Check the current statute for your bracket, and count days carefully.

**My lease has a clause that seems illegal. Does signing mean I'm stuck?**
No. Unenforceable clauses (habitability waivers, illegal fees, waivers of the right to a jury or to organize) are void even if signed.
