# Wills, Trusts, and Probate in Colorado: An Estate Planning Starter Guide

Most adults know they "should have a will" — and most put it off for years. The good news: Colorado's estate laws are relatively straightforward, and a basic plan is more affordable than most people expect. Here's what every Denver adult should understand.

## What Happens If You Die Without a Will?

Dying without a will is called dying **intestate**, and Colorado's intestacy statutes — not you — decide who inherits. The formula depends on which relatives survive you, and it can produce surprising results in blended families, where a surviving spouse may share the estate with children from a prior relationship. Unmarried partners inherit **nothing** under intestacy, no matter how long the relationship.

A will lets you decide who inherits, who administers your estate (your **personal representative**), and — critically for parents — who you nominate as **guardian for your minor children**.

## What Makes a Will Valid in Colorado?

The basics: the person making the will must generally be 18 or older and of sound mind, and the will must be in writing and signed, typically with **two witnesses** or notarization. Colorado also recognizes **holographic (handwritten) wills** in certain circumstances — but DIY wills are a leading source of family litigation. Ambiguous wording, missing signatures, and outdated beneficiaries cost far more to untangle in probate than a properly drafted will costs up front.

## The Documents in a Complete Basic Plan

A solid Colorado estate plan usually includes four documents:

1. **Will** — distributes property, names your personal representative and guardians
2. **Financial (general durable) power of attorney** — authorizes someone to handle your finances if you're incapacitated
3. **Medical durable power of attorney** — names your healthcare decision-maker
4. **Living will (advance directive)** — states your wishes about life-sustaining treatment

Incapacity planning matters as much as death planning: without powers of attorney, your family may need a court-ordered guardianship or conservatorship just to pay your bills or make medical decisions.

## Do You Need a Trust?

A **revocable living trust** holds your assets during your life and distributes them at death without court involvement. Trusts are worth considering when you:

- Own real estate in **more than one state** (avoiding multiple probates)
- Want **privacy** (probate filings are public; trusts are not)
- Have **minor children or beneficiaries** who shouldn't inherit outright at 21
- Have a **blended family** or a beneficiary with special needs
- Want to plan for **incapacity** with minimal disruption

That said, Colorado's probate process is less burdensome than in some states, so not everyone needs a trust. Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance, plus beneficiary deeds for Colorado real estate, can transfer many assets outside probate on their own — just keep them updated, because **beneficiary designations override your will**.

## How Probate Works in Denver

Probate in Denver runs through **Denver Probate Court** (a dedicated court unique to Denver; other counties handle probate in district court). Colorado offers three tracks:

- **Small estate affidavit:** Estates under a modest statutory threshold with no real property can often skip court entirely — heirs collect assets by affidavit.
- **Informal probate:** The common track for uncontested estates with a valid will; minimal court supervision.
- **Formal probate:** Court-supervised, used when there are disputes, an unclear will, or other complications.

Most estates take **six months to a year** to administer. Creditors get a claim period, taxes must be addressed, and the personal representative must inventory assets and account to beneficiaries.

## When to Update Your Plan

Review your estate plan after every major life event: marriage, divorce, births, deaths, a significant change in assets, a move to or from Colorado — and at least every three to five years regardless.
